The CRA 24/72 reporting workflow: roles, triggers, and limits

By Beacon Community. General information, not legal advice; verify current official guidance.

The Cyber Resilience Act creates specific reporting duties for manufacturers for actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents affecting product security. A vulnerability disclosure program and a case-management tool can support preparation, but neither replaces legal assessment or the EU Single Reporting Platform.

Short answer: since 11 September 2026, manufacturers must report qualifying actively exploited vulnerabilities (AEVs) and severe incidents having an impact on product security through ENISA’s Single Reporting Platform (SRP): an early warning within 24 hours, a notification within 72 hours, then a trigger-specific final report. These are maximum outer limits; the law also requires notification without undue delay.

Scope warning. This is a dated operational overview, not legal advice or a determination that a product or organization is in scope. Applicability, role, awareness time, evidence, and required fields need case-specific assessment by the manufacturer.

Who has the reporting duty—and when?

The CRA's relevant manufacturer reporting obligations have applied since 11 September 2026. The European Commission's reporting page distinguishes manufacturer notifications for actively exploited vulnerabilities (AEVs) and severe incidents having an impact on product security. The duty is not triggered by every incoming vulnerability report, by a CVSS score alone, or simply because a product has a vulnerability. The manufacturer must assess whether the legal trigger and applicable scope are met, and when it became aware. CRA Article 24(3) separately brings certain open-source software stewards into reporting obligations from 11 December 2027; whether a particular steward and activity fall within that provision requires a separate scope assessment.

What are the 24-, 72-hour, and final-report deadlines?

  1. Early warning: within 24 hours of becoming aware of an actively exploited vulnerability or severe incident having an impact on product security, where the CRA reporting trigger applies.
  2. Notification: within 72 hours of awareness, with the information required at that stage. The early warning and notification are separate time-bound stages, not a reason to delay escalation while facts are gathered.
  3. Final report: for an AEV, within 14 days after a corrective or mitigating measure is available; for a severe incident, within one month after the 72-hour notification. Check the current legal text and ENISA instructions for the exact case and required contents.

For a simple example, if the manufacturer becomes aware at 10:00 on Monday, the outer limits for early warning and notification fall at 10:00 Tuesday and 10:00 Thursday respectively; the team must still act without undue delay. The AEV final-report clock starts when a corrective or mitigating measure becomes available. The severe-incident final-report clock runs from submission of the 72-hour notification. These dates are an illustration, not legal deadline computation advice.

Manufacturers submit through the EU Single Reporting Platform (SRP), which routes the notification to the designated coordinating CSIRT and makes it available to ENISA under the CRA process. Required fields vary by notification type and stage; do not wait for every fact before escalating.

What counts as an actively exploited vulnerability?

ENISA's SRP FAQ describes an AEV as one for which there is reliable evidence that a malicious actor has exploited it in a system without the system owner's permission. This is an evidence-sensitive legal/operational assessment; a reporter's assertion, proof of concept, or theoretical exploitability is not automatically the same thing. Preserve evidence and escalate promptly rather than making the classification based on a label alone.

How do disclosure, CRA reporting, the SRP, and Beacon differ?

  • Vulnerability disclosure: a researcher or user reports a suspected weakness to the organization. The organization acknowledges, assesses, coordinates and remediates according to its policy.
  • CRA reporting: the manufacturer evaluates the statutory trigger and, when required, files the appropriate notification through the SRP within the relevant deadline.
  • Single Reporting Platform: ENISA’s reporting system for designated mandatory notification workflows; it is not a vulnerability case-management product.
  • Beacon: self-hosted workflow software for receiving and coordinating reports, messages, triage, assignments and records. Beacon is not the SRP, does not file regulatory notifications, determine CRA scope, monitor a product, or guarantee compliance.

Readiness checklist before an incident

  1. Identify which legal entity is the manufacturer for each product and who can make/approve the reporting assessment.
  2. Define an escalation route that is monitored outside ordinary office hours if your risk and obligations require it.
  3. Record when the organization became aware, what evidence supports the trigger assessment, who decided, and why.
  4. Prepare a process to gather product/version, affected components, impact, exploit evidence, mitigation, and required contact details without waiting for a complete investigation.
  5. Ensure an authorized person can access and use the SRP; test the process using ENISA's permitted methods, not a fabricated live incident.
  6. Reconcile the reporter's coordination process with safety, active exploitation, customer obligations and statutory deadlines.
  7. Review official guidance and the legal text at the time of each incident; assign counsel or a competent regulatory owner.
